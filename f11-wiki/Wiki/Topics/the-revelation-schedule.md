# The Revelation Schedule

**The player sees everything through the eyes of characters inside the games.** There is no outside
view, no narrator standing above the realms, and no cutaway to the studio. Whatever the four
developers do, the player meets it the way an inhabitant meets it: as an act of a god.

The mystery unfolds along **two ladders at once**, and neither moves quickly.

## Overview

**Nothing is ever explained; things are re-interpreted.** Every rung on both ladders is a *re-reading*
of evidence the player already has, not a new fact handed over. That is what makes the pacing possible
across eight to ten chapters ([[chapter-based-release]]) — the same events keep meaning something
different.

## Ladder One — Who They Are

| Stage | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 1 | Nothing. The realms have always been sealed; there is no eleventh faction. |
| 2 | **Two gods at war**, with two more switching sides between them. |
| 3 | **Four gods**, in a four-way argument that has no sides ([[the-four-gods]]). |
| 4 | **Four developers**, working against each other and arguing about problems ([[the-four-developers]]). |

**Stage two is the important one and the easiest to skip.** The obvious first reading of the material
is a straight duality — a god of light against a god of darkness, with two lesser powers defecting
between them. It is exactly what a realm would conclude, it is what the scriptures say, and it is
wrong in an instructive way: it makes the Red's and the Blue's switching look like treachery rather
than like two people trying different things.

## Ladder Two — What They Want

| Stage | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Simple, and either malevolent or protective.** A destroyer and a preserver, with two lieutenants. |
| 2 | Motives that do not fit the simple reading — a destroyer who stops when asked, a preserver whose protection is killing things. |
| 3 | **Nuance.** Every one of the four is trying to help, and each is failing in a different direction. |

The second ladder is the one that carries the ending, because the conclusion the protagonists reach —
*no single god is completely right and none is completely wrong* — is unreachable from stage one.

## The Corrections That Land Late

Three facts the player will hold backwards for most of the game, and each is a scheduled reversal:

- **The Black did not link the realms out of spite.** He argued that the stagnant world should be
  connected and **won the argument** ([[the-linking-of-the-realms]]). The most destructive act in
  history was a reasonable proposal that carried the room.
- **The White did not seal the realms to imprison anyone.** He argued that closing the ways would
  **stabilise** them, and won ([[the-long-disconnection]]).
- **The first genuinely illegitimate act was the Red's**, not either senior's — a small change made
  without proposal or review, which became the catalyst for the Blue's much larger ones.

So the two figures the realms cast as the great antagonists are the two who did things **properly**,
and the wrong turn was taken by the one nobody suspects.

## The Gods Fighting Is The Devs Arguing

The correspondence is direct and it is the engine of the whole mystery: **when the realms see their
gods at war, four friends are arguing about how to fix something.** Divine conflict, read correctly,
is a disagreement between people who all want the same outcome.

This is what the player is being trained to notice across the whole game, and it is why the reveal can
land without a scene at the studio: by the time the last rung arrives, the player has watched dozens
of divine acts that only ever made sense as workplace disagreements.
